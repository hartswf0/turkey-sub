Prompts as Constitutions: The Praxis of Constrained Worldmaking

A unified abstract, studio one-pager, and line-by-line bibliographic verification tying together PAELEO, Oulipo and Ouxpo, the SCION Screening Room, Dungeons & Dragons (LEGOS / Trout Town), and RIPPLES as as-built instruments of one research program.

Abstract

Ready for conference submission, monograph framing, or lab overview (228 words).

Contemporary generative media operates through what Hannes Bajohr (2024) terms operative ekphrasis: language no longer describes a prior visual object, but causally compiles images, scenes, and behaviors. Left unconstrained, however, stochastic models optimize for surface plausibility at the expense of structural memory, material provenance, and falsifiability. Rooms drift, style filters counterfeit physical craft, and single-loop agents validate their own hallucinations. PAELEO (Promptable Augmented Environments Lab · Language for Ekphrastic Operations) addresses this epistemic failure by treating prompts not as descriptive wishes, but as falsifiable constitutions for worlds. Drawing on Gregory Bateson's definition of information as a difference that makes a difference, PAELEO operationalizes four distinct regimes of rule, contingency, and memory across a single studio praxis: (1) deterministic syntactic constraint inherited from the Oulipo and Ouxpo (anti-chance); (2) adversarial multi-agent film assembly in SCION, governed by an open-archive and physical-craft material vow (foraged or handmade, never faked) and a blind critic empowered to fail unintelligible cuts; (3) bounded polyhedral risk in Dungeons & Dragons and LEGOS, where chance, memory, and consequence act as ligaments binding static scene elements into irreversible play; and (4) live nonhuman state perturbation in RIPPLES, where a World Jockey steers ecological worldtext without plot or victory. Across all four instruments, meaning emerges only where a system can visibly resist, remember, and say no.

Keywords: operative ekphrasis · constrained generation · Oulipo and Ouxpo · multi-agent governance · tabletop state machines · nonhuman worldtext · archival provenance

Studio One-Pager: The Idea and the Five-Act Praxis

The central idea: Unconstrained prompting asks a statistical model to improvise both the law of a world and its surface appearance at the same time, which makes drift and self-congratulation inevitable. Your work separates world constitution (the formal rule, grid, or state sheet), material trace (credited archive or handmade craft), perturbation (anti-chance, dice, or vector injection), and judgment (blind readers, swapped player sheets, or tick logs). That separation turns generative AI and collaborative play from black-box spectacle into a disciplined studio practice.

ACT 1

Freeze a Small Baseline World

Hold the dramatic seed tiny and constant so formal operations become visible: one railway-station room with five objects across 17 workshops and 99 Ways, one domestic cupboard on an 8x6 grid in RIPPLES, or five LEGOS slots in Trout Town.

ACT 2

Bind Syntax Before Expression

Never instruct a human or agent to "be creative." Lock a structural constraint on the medium's actual joints: ban a vowel as helmet air drops, cut film shots to Fibonacci frame counts, fold a comic strip on a physical crease, or lock prose inside chemical gradients.

ACT 3

Enforce Material Humility

Refuse synthetic stock footage and style-filter mimicry. Every element must be foraged from credited open-license archives (2 to 19 sources per cut), handmade with paper, ink, and scissors, or marked with explicit epistemic uncertainty in worldtext.

ACT 4

Calibrate the Perturbation Dial

Select how contingency enters the state machine: 0% dice in Oulipian anti-chance, quarantined assembly in SCION, 1d20 or 1d6 risk ligaments in D&D and LEGOS, or live GOAL / OBSTACLE / SHIFT vector cascades in RIPPLES.

ACT 5

Firewall Maker from Referee

Builders never grade their own work. SCION routes every cut through unbriefed blind readers, a license auditor, and an independent critic (16/25 non-passing cuts stay public); Trout Town swaps sheets between pairs; RIPPLES writes an immutable [TICK] log.

Bibliographic Verification: Six Concrete Fixes Before Publication

Checking every workshop, film, novel, and comic strip on your Screening Room page against French national filmographies (Unifrance, INA, BnF), publisher catalogs (Gallimard, L'Association, Fluide Glacial), and Oulipo/Ouxpo histories confirms that the architecture is overwhelmingly solid, while surfacing six exact scholarly corrections.

1. La Machine à tuer le temps (c. 1974)

Unattested film title. François Le Lionnais launched Oucinépo in 1974 (renamed Oucipo in 1997), and Queneau used the phrase tuer le temps in Les Derniers Jours (1936), but no 1974 Queneau/Oucipo film or published text carries this title in Gallimard or BnF catalogs. Cite the exact Oulipo archive minute if from a private 1974 circular, or relabel as an Oucinépo 1974 fixed-pool reconstruction.

2. Teuf-Teuf: 1955, not 1956

One-year date slip. Your card lists Jean Jabely's animated short Teuf-Teuf (commentary by Raymond Queneau, voice by Jean Richard, Les Films Armorial) as 1956. Both Unifrance and French 16mm registries (BD-Ciné) record its production year as 1955 (while Jabely's Bang Bang is confirmed 1958).

3. Oupolpot vs. Animated Exercises

16 of 17 workshops are historical. Oupolpot (Ouvroir de politique potentielle, 2001) is an attested Ouxpo workshop (correcting our earlier draft note). Only Exercices de style · Animated is a studio benchmark prompt rather than a named Ouxpo collective (the historical animation workshop is OuAniPo).

4. Missing Oubapo Dates on 3 Cards

Add publication years for parity. Three Étienne Lécroart cards omit dates while neighboring cards show them: Boucle-là ! appeared in 2000 (Libération & Oupus 3, spelled Boucle-la ! on Lécroart's site), Quatre-vingt-quinze in 2003 (Bang ! n°4, 95 paths), and Les Caïds de la gaudriole in 2007 (Fluide Glacial).

5. Perec Text vs. Screen Dates

Keep text/film dates explicit. Un homme qui dort is a 1967 novel and 1974 Prix Jean-Vigo film; Les Lieux d'une fugue adapts a 1965 text into a 1978 INA broadcast (shot 1976); Récits d'Ellis Island (1980) was filmed 1978–1979 with Robert Bober (Assoc. Perec).

6. Le Maki Mococo Dual Credit

Clarify poem vs. DVD date. Jacques Roubaud's poem Le Maki Mococo appeared in Les Animaux de personne (Seghers, 1991); Jean-Christophe Ribot's documentary DVD (with Yvanne Chenouf, AFL) films classroom readings of that poem.
